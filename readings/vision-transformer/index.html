How does a model built to read learn to see?

Deep Learning

Point your phone camera at a dog, and an app tells you the breed. Ask a chatbot to fix an email, and it rewrites it in seconds. These two tasks look nothing alike. One works with pixels, the other with words. Yet today both can run on the same kind of neural network.

That network is called the Transformer. It was born in 2017 at Google, and its first job was translation. Its authors made a bold choice: no recurrence and no convolutions. The model reads a whole sentence at once, and every word looks at every other word to decide what matters. They called this mechanism attention, and the paper's title said it all: "Attention Is All You Need." It beat the best translation systems of its time, and it trained in three and a half days on eight GPUs[1].

For a few years, images stayed with convolutional networks, which were designed for pictures. Then, in 2020, another team at Google tried something that sounded strange. They cut a photo into small squares of 16 by 16 pixels and gave those squares to a Transformer, as if each one were a word in a sentence. The paper's title was a joke with a point: "An Image is Worth 16x16 Words." The model had no built-in idea of what an image is. It did not know that neighboring squares belong together. With enough training data, it learned that on its own and matched the best convolutional networks[2].

A year later, researchers at Facebook AI Research and Inria noticed something even more surprising. They trained a Vision Transformer without telling it what was in any photo. No labels at all. When they looked at where the model was paying attention, it had learned to outline the main object in each image, separating a dog or a bird from the background[3]. Nobody had taught it what an object is.

This is why the same idea now shows up in translation, chatbots, image search, and medical imaging. Attention turned out to be a general tool, not a trick for language.

But it raises a puzzle. A model that only compares squares with other squares, and never sees the image as a grid, somehow learns to find the dog and ignore the grass. How can attention alone discover what matters in an image?
